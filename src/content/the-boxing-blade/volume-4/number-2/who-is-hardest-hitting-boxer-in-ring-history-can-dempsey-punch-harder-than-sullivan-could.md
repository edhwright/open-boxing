# Who Is Hardest Hitting Boxer in Ring History? Can Dempsey Punch Harder Than Sullivan Could?

## Experts Take Different Sides on This Question and It Looks Like Hard Matter to Decide Just Which is Right—Jim Coffroth Favors Present Champion But Al Spink Maintains That Old John L. Was the Greater Hitter

Few men today have a better line on the old and new pugilists than Jim Coffroth of San Francisco.

Having for years been the leading fight club manager of the Pacific Coast and having in that time had dealings with all the big and little pugilists of the past three decades Coffroth has had a fine chance to size them all up and discover just what there was in each of them.

Assisting him, too, in this direction was his fine eye sight, his splendid knowledge of human nature and his thorough mastery of the boxing game in all its ramifications.

It is because of all this that Coffroth should be the best judge of a champion of any man living.

Only the other day in sizing up all the great fighters that have come and gone since Sullivan's time Coffroth put Jeffries and the present Jack Dempsey at the very head of the class.

Coffroth thinks that no man of all the great American pugilists could hit as hard a blow as Dempsey and he bases his opinion probably on the fearful blows that Dempsey showered on Jess Willard at Toledo.

If any one of the old fighters compared with Dempsey then, according to Coffroth, that man was Jeffries. He does not seem to consider John L. Sullivan in the same class as those two.

Here is where Coffroth and I cannot agree for according to my honest belief Sullivan in his prime could have whipped all the big and little fighters that have come since his time and performed the task easily.

No one could judge of Sullivan's real skill and speed as a fighter except one who saw him in the zenith of his power.

Many who saw Jeffries fall before Johnson at Reno charged that Jeffries was never a fighter.

And their verdict might be likened to the claim of those who never saw Sullivan until he had reached that point where the epigastrium had commenced to expand and when his speed, skill and strength as a pugilist had vanished.

Not one of the writers who belittle the skill of Sullivan ever saw him in his first fight for the championship.

Ergo, not one of them can write faithfully of the strength and skill of the Boston boy when he was at the top rung of his career.

The statement that he won the championship from Paddy Ryan when the latter was 52 years of age and played out is easily proven false and When he met Sullivan he was in his thirtieth year.

On February 7, 1882, when Ryan met Sullivan, he was in appearance well worthy of the name of Troy Giant He stood over six feet in height, weighed 205 pounds, and was magnificently built and muscled. Added to this was the prestige he carried as champion.

Sullivan on this seventh day of February, 1882, when he faced the champion, was in his twenty-fourth year.

But he was noted even at this time for his prodigious strength and the knowing ones before the fight offered $100 to $80 that John L. would defeat the Trojan.

In my long career as boxing writer I have never seen before or since a pugilist who I thought could compare with Sullivan as I saw him that day at Mississippi City.

Mighty men since that long ago have tried for the heavyweight championship.

The men who have held it in turn since then are Corbett, Fitzsimmons, Jeffries, Johnson, Willard and Dempsey.

I have seen all these men when they were at the very top crest of their careers and I do not believe that any one of them could have defeated Sullivan as he was that day when he met and defeated Ryan for the world's championship.

But it was his wonderful speed, combined with his size and weight, that would have made Sullivan more than a match for any of the champions that have come since.

As a boxer he was not only skillful and speedy, but he was ambidextrous and he could strike a fearful blow with either hand.

But added to that he seemed a natural born fighter.
