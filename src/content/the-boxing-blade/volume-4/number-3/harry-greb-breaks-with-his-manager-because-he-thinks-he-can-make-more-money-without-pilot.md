# Harry Greb Breaks With His Manager Because He Thinks He Can Make More Money Without Pilot

## Pittsburgh Fighter Says George Engel Doesn't Get Him Enough Work and That He Can Keep Busier by Handling His Own Business—Also Wants to Save Cut Paid to Manager—Tom Andrews Says He Had Better Forget About Fighting Dempsey—Timely Ring Gossip

Milwaukee—Harry Greb's anxiety to annex an independent fortune before he gets through with the boxing game no doubt had considerable to do with his breaking away from his manager, George Engel, Harry thinks he can dispense with the services of a manager and save himself the 33 1-3 per cent which he has been paying out. At least, that is the impression Harry gave the writer in a chat at Madison Square Garden, New York a few days ago. There was some talk of Harry going back with his former manager, James Mason of Pittsburgh, but this the light heavyweight champion would not confirm nor would he deny it outright.

"Mr. Engel was not aggressive enough for me," said Greb, "and did not keep me as busy as I should have been. I thrive on plenty of work and George did not get the matches I expected. I made money, but could have made much more. I want about four or five matches before taking on Gene Tunney in a championship bout, as I want to be in the best of shape when I meet him. I need a few bouts to put me on edge. I intend to fulfill the contracts Mr. Engel made for me and will then bid him goodbye."

Greb has been challenging Jack Dempsey to a heavyweight match but if he is wise he will forget it or get himself a manager who will keep him away from the big champion unless Harry wants to take a beating for what he would get out of it. As a middle and light heavyweight Harry Greb is a great battler, but when it comes to Dempsey in the heavyweight division,—well, just forget it.

Greb does not appear in the best of shape at present, due no doubt to worry over the serious illness of his wife. Besides he has had cuts over both eyes and he will be wise to have them thoroughly healed and cared for before engaging in even "soft matches."

* * *

When one reads of the big money being taken in at Madison Square Garden, New York, for boxing shows, the impression is gained that the fighters in that part of the country are just waddling about in the midst of gold coin. A trip along the great white way adds zest to the feeling, but take a run to the outlying districts where boxing shows are held and a different impression prevails.

While on a tour of the various clubs in the big time city recently, the writer bumped into dozens, yes hundreds of boxers, anxious and ready to take a match on a moment's notice. There are just a few of the big clubs where the "easy money" is being grabbed off and mostly by the stars. Such places as the Garden, the big arena in Jersey, one in Brooklyn and the new Arena in Philadelphia, with another in Boston are about the only ones where the big coin is gathered in, with the Garden leading all of them. Outside of the above, the smaller clubs are drawing just fairly good crowds and the boxers are lucky to get enough to keep them in pork and beans.

At the big clubs, however, it is a sure treat to watch the big crowds. The fans want some kind of amusement and they turn out in goodly numbers for anything that is half good. Joe Welling and a youngster named Andy Thomas drew $24,000 at the Garden, while the week before Pancho Villa and Abe Goldstein packed away over $48,000 and turned thousands away.

* * *

The big noise in the East just now, outside of a couple of champions, is Pancho Villa, the little Filipino, who recently won the flyweight title from Johnny Buff and since that time has defeated some of the best lads in the East. His defeat of Goldstein and also of Young Montreal stamps him as a marvel, as he gave away plenty of weight in each match. It takes a mighty good bantam to beat Montreal and Villa turned the trick at Boston, and in what might be termed Montreal's home town at that. The fans now want Villa and Frankie Genaro for a championship battle at the Garden, and if it is arranged it will draw over $75,000.

* * *

Another popular lad in New York just now is "Irish" Johnny Curtin. He belongs in Jersey, but New York has adopted him. Even Mayor Hague of Jersey City takes a great interest in him. He is 24 years old and has had a lot of experience, but never had much of a chance to get out of the prelim class until the past year. They think he is a counterpart of the once famous Terry McGovern.

The new arena in Jersey City, known as the Arena A. C., will be opened in December with "Irish" Johnny Curtin and Midget Smith. The Jersey people are planning big things and hope before long to compete with New York in the holding of championship matches. When the Jersey legislature convenes after the Christmas holidays, an amendment providing for 15-round decision bouts will be introduced. It is proposed too, to raise the price limit to $25 against $15 in New York, which would give Tex Rickard the edge in holding big championship events there. It is claimed that the amendment has a good chance of being put through.

* * *

It is understood that Jack Curley, wrestling impresario, has made an offer of $25,000 to Eugene Criqui, French star, to meet Johnny Dundee, junior lightweight champion of America, for a match at 126 pounds, the featherweight limit. It is not likely that Dundee will agree to such a match, for the reason that he finds it hard just now to make even 130 pounds. It seemed easy some time ago for Johnny to get down to 126, but he probably made a mistake in doing it. Criqui would have a good chance of stopping the clever little Italian at that poundage. Dundee announces that he has been promised a guarantee of $35,000 for three fights in Australia, at the Sydney Stadium, but he is skeptical about taking it. Johnny says it would be O. K. for one battle but he balks at three as he can make that in the United States without going away and losing time and in short contests.

* * *

Leo P. Flynn, manager of many stars, is out with a challenge to Mickey Walker for the welterweight title, on behalf of Dave Shade. "Dave has knocked out Walker, which others have not done," says Leo, "and for that reason should get first crack at the championship. We have the necessary $2,500 forfeit ready for Walker any time he says he is willing to go. Mr. Tendler can also have his game any old time, but at the welterweight limit of 147 pounds, which was the weight for the proposed Milwaukee match some time ago, and not 144 as the promoters advertised it. The same goes, too, for Pinky Mitchell of Milwaukee. I will say that Pinky gave Tendler a hard battle and that Shade beat him, but understand that he wants another chance. Well, Pinky can have it any time he feels like it
