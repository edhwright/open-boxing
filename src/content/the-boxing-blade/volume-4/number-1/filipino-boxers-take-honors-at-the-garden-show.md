# Filipino Boxers Take Honors at the Garden Show

## Abe Attel Goldstein Refuses to Make Fight of It with Pancho Villa in Main Event—Jack Bernstein Outpoints Elino Flores in 12 Rounds but Fighting Spirit of Little Filipino Makes Him Hero of Show—Eastern Boxing News

New York City—Not since the memorable Jack Sharkey-Joe Lynch hurricane encounter, the second show staged in Madison Square Garden after the enactment of the Walker Law, was a greater fight seen than the Jack Bernstein and Elino Flores 12-rounder which supplied many thrills to 15,000 spectators crowded into every nook and corner of Tex Rickard's amphitheatre on the evening of Nov. 16. This happened to be the semi-windup to the Pancho Villa-Abe Goldstein bout and it saved the show.

Let's forget the main event and continue where we left off on Messrs. Bernstein and Flores. Three days before the scrap Flores complained to his manager, Frank Churchill, that he had stomach trouble and Doc Hirsch came all the way down from Yonkers to inform matchmaker Frank Flournoy his charge had a heavy cold. For a time it looked like one or the other would have to cancel the engagement.

Can you imagine what would have happened in the Garden ring had both boys been in the pink of condition. They'd have had to call in the police to separate them. Right off the reel they went about the business of trying to score a knock out. Bernstein got his mighty left hook into play and it never missed its mark. Flores knew he was in a battle but he made the most of it and shot in right hooks to the body and right crosses to the face as fast as his little arms would work.

The crowd was on its feet most of the twelve rounds. The house was in an uproar and you could have heard the noise as far as Times Square, so terrific was the din in appreciation of the efforts of the gladiators. Bernstein piled up points in each succeeding session so that at the end he was the rightful winner but the two judges and referee agreed on a draw. That the decision was not well taken was indicated by the noise which followed its rendition so that announcer Joe Humphreys could not speak his piece on the last bout. The local writers are waging a campaign to have the judges thrown out and leave it to the referee henceforth to decide the issue. This seems like a good thing because right along some very bad verdicts have been turned in.

Flores was a mass of bruises and Bernstein fresh as a daisy when it was all over. Elino, who is the lightweight champion of the Philippines and stablemate of Villa's, made many friends by his game exhibition and the applause he received when he got out of the ring must have tickled his heart. He kept plunging in while Bernstein rammed him with an assortment of lefts and rights.

Bernstein became a hero overnight and his services are in demand everywhere. He has come up from nowhere into a position where he can make Johnny Dundee defend his 130 pounds championship. It is very likely that Flournoy will put on this pair again inside of a month. Flores needs time to have his wounds heal. His victory over Pal Moran recently more than anything else put Bernstein into the limelight.

Goldstein had weight, reach, height and every other conceivable handicap over Villa but he wouldn't fight and practically killed himself as a card around these parts. Everybody felt sorry for his handler, Willie Lewis, who implored him to annihilate the little bit of a thing in front of him but Abie stood pat and refused to budge. Willie came near throwing the water pail on his head so angry was he at the tactics employed by his man. Pancho would stick his chin out to Abie and get himself all wrangled up in the corners giving Goldie many a chance to hit him, but in every case Abe would stand there and put his hands behind his back. It was a miserable exhibition on the part of Goldstein and he is as good as forgotten.

Villa weighed in at 110 pounds at 2 o'clock on the day of the contest and Goldstein tipped the beam at 115½. He looked in perfect condition but once he entered the ring he forgot all about his fighting and posed around. Most of the spectators left before the thing was over. What they had to say about Goldie isn't fit for publication. We feel sorry for Lewis but if a fighter can't fight what's the use of having him around. Better get him a job in the subway, Will.

Villa has accumulated over $30,000 since coming here from Manila. His next bout will be with Frankie Genaro in Newark, 12 rounds, no decision, the latter part of this month. If they ever clash in the Garden to a decision it will be another sell out. The house for Goldstein and Villa was sold out at 11 o'clock on the morning of the fight and the speculators made more money than they ever dreamed of earning. They sold the $7.70 ringside seats, bad ones at that, at $20 apiece and got fancy prices for arena and balcony tickets. They had to call police reserves to keep the crowd in check outside the show house at 8 o'clock and at one time the mob rushed the gates. You never saw such a mob of people before trying to get in to see a fight. Nobody could understand it. The cops had their hands full right up to the time Goldie and Villa clambered through the ropes.

* * *

Two other bouts were enjoyed immensely. Hughey Hutchinson knocked out Eddie James in the last half minute of their six round affair. Hughey knocked James down twice. He had James in a bad way after the third session. James' exhibition of gameness got him a big hand. In the curtain raiser of four rounds Al Brown, the shifty east side featherweight making his first fight in several months, trounced Jackie Harris, of Brooklyn. Al scored a knockdown just before the bell clanged in the second stanza. The guy who rooted hardest for Brown was Francis, assistant booster for the Garden. He swallowed a hunk of tobacco when Al knocked Harris down.

Here's the receipts:

Although no title was at stake the fans turned out in such large numbers that over 15,000 of them were seated in the big inclosure when the lads stepped to the centre of the ring. There were 13,254 fans who paid for tickets. The gross receipts, including the government tax of 10 per cent, amounted to $56,199. The net receipts were $48,535.50, while the state drew down five per cent, which gave it $2,554.50. Villa got 30 per cent of the net receipts of $48,535.50, which made his end $14,560.50, while Goldstein battled for 10 per cent of the net receipts, which made his end $4,853.50. * * *

There is no truth in the report that Harry Greb has given George Engle the gate. George will remain as the Pittsburgh mauler's manager as long as he gets him plenty of work. Greb revels in work. He hates to lay off. Engle is around the Garden every day seeking opponents for his charge. He may get in soon with either Gene Tunney or Tom Gibbons. Since he dethroned Tunney, Greb finds it hard to get fellows to go in a ring with him.

* * *

Jimmy Kelley, entirely recovered from a long siege of illness, is anxious to match Terry Martin with Villa at 116 pounds. What a whale of a fight this would make. They are almost the same type and you'd see many gloves flying around and punches galore scored. This is what I call a real match. Martin hasn't lost a fight since coming under Kelley's wing and he is accounted to be one of the best bantams hereabouts. Why doesn't Joe Lynch give him another fight? Maybe Joe is afraid of losing his title.

* * *

Floyd Johnson, Charley Cook's heavyweight, is ill and Charley has cancelled several good matches. He will be ready to start where he left off inside of a couple of weeks.
